Introduction

Counting techniques are a class of functions that are not algebraic, meaning they cannot be expressed as a finite combination of algebraic operations. These functions are essential in discrete mathematics and have many applications in various fields. In this tutorial, we will explore the properties and applications of counting techniques, including product rule, sum rule, permutations, combinations, and principle of inclusion-exclusion.

PRODUCT RULE

The product rule is a fundamental counting technique that allows us to count the number of ways to choose two or more items from a set. It is used to count the number of ways to choose two items from a set, the number of ways to choose three items from a set, and so on.

Key Point: The product rule is a fundamental counting technique that allows us to count the number of ways to choose two or more items from a set.
Problem 1
Calculate the number of 3-digit numbers that one can form using the odd digits.
  1. Identify the set of digits: The odd digits are 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. There are 5 possible digits.
  2. Determine constraints for a 3-digit number: The first digit (hundreds place) cannot be zero. Since all odd digits are non-zero, this condition is automatically satisfied.
  3. Repetition is allowed: The problem does not specify distinct digits, so digits can be repeated. Each digit position (hundreds, tens, units) can be filled with any of the 5 odd digits.
  4. Calculate the total number:
    \[ \text{Number of choices} = 5 \times 5 \times 5 = 5^3 = 125 \]
  5. Conclusion: There are 125 possible 3-digit numbers.
Problem 2
Calculate the number of ways of choosing 2 boys from 6 boys and 3 girls from 7 girls.
    Is this a product rule question?
    Yes! This problem is a classic example of the product rule in discrete mathematics. The product rule states that if one event can occur in $m$ ways and a second independent event can occur in $n$ ways, then the two events together can occur in $m \times n$ ways. Here, choosing boys and choosing girls are independent choices, so we multiply the number of ways for each.
  1. Choosing boys: The number of ways to choose 2 boys from 6 boys is given by the combination formula, as order does not matter.
    \[ \binom{6}{2} = \frac{6!}{2!(6-2)!} = \frac{6 \times 5}{2 \times 1} = 15 \]
  2. Choosing girls: The number of ways to choose 3 girls from 7 girls is similarly calculated using combinations.
    \[ \binom{7}{3} = \frac{7!}{3!(7-3)!} = \frac{7 \times 6 \times 5}{3 \times 2 \times 1} = 35 \]
  3. Combine choices (Product Rule): Since the choices of boys and girls are independent, the total number of ways is the product.
    \[ \binom{6}{2} \times \binom{7}{3} = 15 \times 35 = 525 \]
  4. Conclusion: There are 525 possible ways.
Problem 3
Calculate the number of 6-letter words (not necessarily meaningful words) that one can form using the English alphabet such that the word has exactly two vowels.
  1. Understand the alphabet: The English alphabet has 26 letters. Among them, 5 are vowels (a, e, i, o, u), and the remaining 21 are consonants. Since repetition is allowed, any letter can appear more than once in a word.
  2. Choose vowel positions (Product Rule Step 1): To ensure the word has exactly 2 vowels, we first select 2 out of the 6 positions where vowels will be placed. This can be done in:
    \[ \binom{6}{2} = \frac{6!}{2!(6-2)!} = \frac{6 \times 5}{2 \times 1} = 15 \text{ ways} \]
    Why do we need this step?
    Even though repetition is allowed and we don’t care about unique letters, we must still choose the positions where the vowels go. That’s because different arrangements of the same letters (like "AEBCDF" vs "EABCDF") count as different 6-letter words. So choosing which two of the six positions will contain vowels is essential to count all valid arrangements accurately. But if your lecturer/instructor tells you to ignore this fact, Remember to always follow.
  3. Fill vowel positions (Product Rule Step 2): For each of the 2 vowel positions, any of the 5 vowels can be used (with repetition). So:
    \[ \text{Vowel choices} = 5 \times 5 = 5^2 = 25 \text{ ways} \]
  4. Fill consonant positions (Product Rule Step 3): The remaining 4 positions must be consonants. Each position can be filled with any of the 21 consonants (again, repetition allowed), so:
    \[ \text{Consonant choices} = 21 \times 21 \times 21 \times 21 = 21^4 \] \[ 21^2 = 441,\quad 21^4 = (21^2)^2 = 441^2 = 194481 \]
  5. Apply the product rule: The total number of valid words is the product of all these independent choices:
    \[ \text{Total} = \binom{6}{2} \times 5^2 \times 21^4 = 15 \times 25 \times 194481 \]
    Compute step by step:
    \[ 15 \times 25 = 375,\quad 375 \times 194481 = 72,930,375 \]
  6. Final Answer: There are exactly 72,930,375 different 6-letter words with exactly two vowels.
Note: If the problem asked for at least two vowels, or distinct vowels only, or no repetition, then additional cases and more advanced techniques would be needed.
Problem 4
A four-digit positive integer is formed using the digits 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 and 9. Calculate the total number of four-digit positive even integers that can be formed.
  1. Digits available: The digits are 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 9. All are non-zero and distinct. The problem implies no repetition, as it specifies "using the digits" without mentioning repetition.
  2. Even integer constraint: A number is even if its last digit (units place) is even. The even digits in the set are 2 and 6. Thus, there are 2 choices for the units place.
  3. First digit constraint: The first digit (thousands place) cannot be zero, but since no digit is zero, any remaining digit can be used.
  4. Assign digits after fixing the units place: After choosing the units digit, 6 digits remain for the thousands, hundreds, and tens places. Since order matters, we assign digits sequentially.

    1. Thousands place: 6 choices (any of the remaining 6 digits).
    2. Hundreds place: 5 choices (any of the remaining 5 digits).
    3. Tens place: 4 choices (any of the remaining 4 digits).
  5. Total even numbers:
    \[ \text{Choices} = (\text{Even}) \times (\text{thousands}) \times (\text{hundreds}) \times (\text{tens}) \] \[= 2 \times (7-1) \times (7-2) \times (7-3) = 2\times 6\times \times 5 \times 4 = 240\]
  6. Conclusion: There are 240 possible four-digit even integers.
Problem 5
MTL Company uses 8-digit telephone numbers where each telephone number is designated to start with the two digits 01. How many 8-digit telephone numbers are possible? [4 marks]
  1. Understand the structure: An 8-digit telephone number that starts with 01 looks like this: 01XXXXXX, where each X is a digit (0–9). The first two digits are fixed as 0 and 1.
  2. Remaining digits: After fixing the first two digits, we are left with 6 positions to fill. Each of these 6 positions can be filled by any of the digits from 0 to 9. That gives:
    \[ 10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 10^6 = 1,000,000 \]
  3. Apply the multiplication principle: Since the choices for each of the remaining 6 digits are independent, we multiply:
    \[ \text{Total Numbers} = 1 \times 10^6 = 1,000,000 \]
  4. Final Answer: There are exactly 1,000,000 different 8-digit telephone numbers that start with 01.
Note:
If the question had asked for all possible 8-digit numbers (without restriction), the total would have been 10^8 = 100,000,000. But because the first two digits are fixed, we only count combinations for the last 6 digits.
Problem 6
Suppose we have seven different courses offered in the morning and five different courses offered in the afternoon for those willing to do a Diploma in Statistics course at Chancellor College. How many choices would one student have if he/she wishes to enroll in one course in the morning and one in the afternoon?
  1. Morning courses: There are 7 different courses, so the number of choices for the morning course is 7.
  2. Afternoon courses: There are 5 different courses, so the number of choices for the afternoon course is 5.
  3. Combine choices: Since the student chooses one course from each time slot independently, the total number of choices is the product.
    \[ \text{Total choices} = 7 \times 5 = 35 \]
  4. Conclusion: The student has 35 possible choices.

SUM RULE

The sum rule (also called the addition principle) is a basic counting technique used when a task can be done in one of several distinct ways. If there are n ways to do task A and m ways to do task B, and the two tasks cannot both occur at the same time, then there are n + m ways to do either task A or task B.

Key Point: The sum rule is used when you have a choice between mutually exclusive options: if one task can be done in n ways and another in m ways (with no overlap), then there are n + m ways to do one or the other.
Problem 1
A cafeteria offers 3 types of sandwiches and 2 types of salads. If you can choose either a sandwich or a salad, how many choices do you have?
  1. Count the number of sandwiches: 3
  2. Count the number of salads: 2
  3. Apply the sum rule:
    \[ \text{Total choices} = 3 + 2 = 5 \]
  4. Conclusion: There are 5 possible choices if you can pick either a sandwich or a salad.
Problem 2
Suppose we have seven different courses offered in the morning and five different courses offered in the afternoon for those willing to do a Diploma in Statistics course at Chancellor College. How many choices would one student have if he/she wishes to enroll in one course only, either in the morning or in the afternoon?
  1. Morning courses: There are 7 different courses offered in the morning, so the number of morning choices is 7.
  2. Afternoon courses: There are 5 different courses offered in the afternoon, so the number of afternoon choices is 5.
  3. Combine choices (Sum Rule): Since the student is to choose only one course total, and it can be from either the morning or afternoon (but not both), we apply the Sum Rule.
    \[ \text{Total choices} = 7 + 5 = 12 \]
  4. Final Answer: The student has 12 possible choices in total.
Note: This problem uses the Sum Rule, since the student selects one course from either the morning session or the afternoon session — but not both. The choices are mutually exclusive, so we add the counts: 7 + 5 = 12.

Combinations and Permutations

Combinations and permutations are two of the most important ideas in counting problems, but they answer different questions:

  • Permutations are used when you want to know how many ways you can arrange a set of objects, where the order matters. For example, arranging 3 books on a shelf: placing Book A, then Book B, then Book C is different from placing Book C, then Book B, then Book A. Every different order counts as a different permutation.
  • Combinations are used when you want to know how many ways you can choose a group of objects, where the order does not matter. For example, if you are picking 3 students from a class to form a team, it does not matter in which order you pick them—choosing Alice, Bob, and Carol is the same as choosing Carol, Bob, and Alice. All that matters is who is chosen, not the order.

In summary: permutations count arrangements (order matters), while combinations count selections (order does not matter).

Combinations and Permutaions
Problem 1
In how many ways can we place three balls coloured red, blue, and white in ten boxes numbered 1 through 10 if each box can hold only one ball at a time?
  1. Identify the problem type: We need to arrange 3 distinct balls in 10 distinct boxes, where order matters.
    This is a permutation problem: selecting and arranging 3 objects from 10 positions.
  2. Apply the permutation formula:
    \[P(n,r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\] where \(n = 10\) (boxes) and \(r = 3\) (balls)
  3. Calculate the permutation:
    \[P(10,3) = \frac{10!}{(10-3)!} = \frac{10!}{7!} = 10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720\]
  4. Alternative approach (step-by-step):
    $\to$ Choose a box for the red ball: 10 choices
    $\to$ Choose a box for the blue ball: 9 remaining choices
    $\to$ Choose a box for the white ball: 8 remaining choices
    $\to$ Total: \(10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720\)
  5. Conclusion: There are 720 ways to place the balls.
Problem 2
In how many ways can three examinations be scheduled within a five-day period so that no two examinations are scheduled on the same day?
  1. Identify the problem type: We need to schedule 3 distinct exams on 5 different days, where order matters.
    This is a permutation problem: selecting and arranging 3 days from 5 available days.
  2. Apply the permutation formula:
    \[P(n,r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\] where \(n = 5\) (days) and \(r = 3\) (exams)
  3. Calculate the permutation:
    \[P(5,3) = \frac{5!}{(5-3)!} = \frac{5!}{2!} = 5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60\]
  4. Alternative approach (step-by-step):
    $\to$ Choose a day for exam 1: 5 choices
    $\to$ Choose a day for exam 2: 4 remaining choices
    $\to$ Choose a day for exam 3: 3 remaining choices
    $\to$ Total: \(5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60\)
  5. Conclusion: There are 60 ways to schedule the examinations.
Problem 3
Determine the number of four-digit decimal numbers that contain no repeated digits.
  1. Identify the problem type: We need to form 4-digit numbers using distinct digits, where position matters.
    This is a permutation problem with restrictions: the first digit cannot be 0.
  2. Handle the restriction:
    $\to$ First digit (thousands place): 9 choices (1-9, cannot be 0)
    $\to$ Remaining three digits: Choose 3 from the remaining 9 digits (including 0)
  3. Apply permutation for remaining positions:
    After choosing the first digit, we have \(P(9,3)\) ways to arrange the remaining 3 digits
    \[P(9,3) = \frac{9!}{(9-3)!} = \frac{9!}{6!} = 9 \times 8 \times 7 = 504\]
  4. Calculate the total:
    \[\text{Total} = 9 \times P(9,3) = 9 \times 504 = 4536\]
    Alternatively: \(9 \times 9 \times 8 \times 7 = 4536\)
  5. Conclusion: There are 4536 four-digit numbers with no repeated digits.
Problem 4
Suppose that we have seven rooms and want to assign four of them to four programmers as offices and use the remaining three rooms for computer terminals. In how many ways can you carry out such an assignment procedure?
  1. Identify the problem type: We need to assign 4 specific programmers to 4 out of 7 rooms, where order matters.
    This is a permutation problem: selecting and arranging 4 rooms from 7 available rooms for distinct programmers.
  2. Apply the permutation formula:
    \[P(n,r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\] where \(n = 7\) (rooms) and \(r = 4\) (programmers)
  3. Calculate the permutation:
    \[P(7,4) = \frac{7!}{(7-4)!} = \frac{7!}{3!} = 7 \times 6 \times 5 \times 4 = 840\]
  4. Alternative approach (step-by-step):
    $\to$ Choose a room for programmer 1: 7 choices
    $\to$ Choose a room for programmer 2: 6 remaining choices
    $\to$ Choose a room for programmer 3: 5 remaining choices
    $\to$ Choose a room for programmer 4: 4 remaining choices
    $\to$ The remaining 3 rooms are automatically assigned to terminals
  5. Conclusion: There are 840 ways to carry out the assignment.
Problem 5
In how many ways can one make up strings of four distinct letters followed by three distinct digits?
  1. Identify the problem type: We need to create strings with two parts: 4 distinct letters and 3 distinct digits.
    This involves two independent permutation problems that we multiply together.
  2. Calculate permutations for the letter part:
    Choose and arrange 4 letters from 26 available letters: \[P(26,4) = \frac{26!}{(26-4)!} = \frac{26!}{22!}\]
    \[P(26,4) = 26 \times 25 \times 24 \times 23 = 358,800\]
  3. Calculate permutations for the digit part:
    Choose and arrange 3 digits from 10 available digits (0-9): \[P(10,3) = \frac{10!}{(10-3)!} = \frac{10!}{7!}\]
    \[P(10,3) = 10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720\]
  4. Apply the multiplication principle:
    \[\text{Total} = P(26,4) \times P(10,3) = 358,800 \times 720 = 258,336,000\]
    Since the letter and digit selections are independent, we multiply the permutations.
  5. Conclusion: There are 258,336,000 such strings.
Problem 6
In how many ways can one make up strings of four distinct letters followed by three distinct digits?
  1. String Structure: The string is formed by two parts:
    • 4 distinct letters (positions 1–4)
    • 3 distinct digits (positions 5–7)
  2. Letter part:
    • There are 26 letters in the English alphabet.
    • We choose 4 distinct letters in order:
      • Position 1: 26 choices
      • Position 2: 25 choices
      • Position 3: 24 choices
      • Position 4: 23 choices
    • Total arrangements:
      \[ 26 \times 25 \times 24 \times 23 = 358,\!800 \]
  3. Digit part:
    • There are 10 digits (0–9).
    • We choose 3 distinct digits in order:
      • Position 5: 10 choices
      • Position 6: 9 choices
      • Position 7: 8 choices
    • Total arrangements:
      \[ 10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720 \]
  4. Total strings (by product rule):
    \[ 358,\!800 \times 720 = 258,\!336,\!000 \]
  5. Conclusion: There are 258,336,000 such strings.
  6. Alternative notation:
    \[ P(26,4) \times P(10,3) \]
Problem 7
In how many ways can one make up strings of four distinct letters followed by three distinct digits?
  1. The string has two parts: four letters followed by three digits. These two sections are independent of each other.
  2. Letter section: There are 26 letters in the English alphabet. Since the letters must be distinct and order matters, the number of ways to select and arrange 4 letters is:
    \[ 26 \times 25 \times 24 \times 23 = 358,\!800 \]
  3. Digit section: There are 10 digits (0–9). The digits must also be distinct and ordered. So, the number of ways to select and arrange 3 digits is:
    \[ 10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720 \]
  4. Final Calculation: By the product rule, the total number of such strings is:
    \[ 358,\!800 \times 720 = 258,\!336,\!000 \]
    This means there are 258,336,000 valid strings.
  5. This can also be expressed using permutation notation:
    \[ P(26,4) \times P(10,3) \]
Problem 8
Calculate the number of four-digit positive integers using the digits 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 9 (no repetition allowed). The condition "even or odd" is always satisfied.
  1. Identify the problem type: We need to form four-digit numbers with distinct digits from a set of 7 non-zero digits. The condition "even or odd" imposes no restrictions.
    This is a permutation problem since order matters and digits are distinct.
  2. Apply the permutation formula:
    \[P(n,r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\] where \(n = 7\) (digits) and \(r = 4\) (positions)
  3. Calculate the permutation:
    \[P(7,4) = 7 \times 6 \times 5 \times 4 = 840\]
  4. Conclusion: There are 840 such four-digit integers.
Problem 8
Determine the number of six-letter sequences from the set \(\{A, B, C, D, E, F, G\}\) (7 distinct letters) such that either the substring "BD" or "BG" appears in order.
  1. Identify the problem type: We form sequences using 6 distinct letters from 7 (omitting one). The substring "BD" or "BG" must appear consecutively.
    We treat "BD" or "BG" as a single block and use permutations with case analysis.
  2. Case analysis:
    • Case 1: Omit D → Letters: \(\{A, B, C, E, F, G\}\)
      Only "BG" can appear. Treat "BG" as one block → 5 entities to arrange: \(5! = 120\)
    • Case 2: Omit G → Letters: \(\{A, B, C, D, E, F\}\)
      Only "BD" can appear. Treat "BD" as one block → 5 entities: \(5! = 120\)
    • Case 3: Omit A, C, E, or F (4 choices) → Letters include both D and G.
      ∙ Arrangements with "BD": \(5! = 120\) (treat "BD" as block)
      ∙ Arrangements with "BG": \(5! = 120\) (treat "BG" as block)
      ∙ Total for one omission: \(120 + 120 = 240\)
      ∙ For 4 omissions: \(4 \times 240 = 960\)
  3. Sum all cases:
    \[\text{Total} = 120 + 120 + 960 = 1200\]
  4. Conclusion: There are 1200 such sequences.
Problem 9
A class has 10 girls and 8 boys. Two winners of the same sex are chosen for a prize (unordered pair).
  1. Identify the problem type: We need combinations of two girls or two boys (mutually exclusive cases).
  2. Calculate combinations:
    \[ \begin{align*} \text{Two girls:} & \quad \binom{10}{2} = \frac{10 \times 9}{2} = 45 \\ \text{Two boys:} & \quad \binom{8}{2} = \frac{8 \times 7}{2} = 28 \end{align*} \]
  3. Apply the addition principle:
    \[\text{Total} = 45 + 28 = 73\]
  4. Conclusion: There are 73 ways to choose the winners.

Select one junior representative (from 52 students) and one senior representative (from 49 students). Roles are distinct.

Binomial and Multinomial Coefficients
Problem 12
Calculate the coeffitient of \(x^3y^2\) in the expansion of \((2x + 3y)^5\).
  1. Identify the binomial expansion: The general term in the expansion of \((a + b)^n\) is given by: \[ T_k = \binom{n}{k} a^{n-k} b^k \]
  2. Set parameters: Here, \(a = 2x\), \(b = 3y\), and \(n = 5\).
  3. Find \(k\) for \(x^3y^2\): We need \(n-k = 3\) and \(k = 2\).
  4. Calculate the coefficient: \[ T_2 = \binom{5}{2} (2x)^3 (3y)^2 \] Calculate each part: \[ \binom{5}{2} = 10, \quad (2x)^3 = 8x^3, \quad (3y)^2 = 9y^2 \]
  5. Combine results: \[ T_2 = 10 \cdot 8x^3 \cdot 9y^2 = 720x^3y^2 \]
  6. Conclusion: The coefficient of \(x^3y^2\) is 720.
Problem 13
Calculate the coefficient of \(x^2y^3z^4\) in the expansion of \((x + 2y + 3z)^9\).
  1. The multinomial expansion is given by: \[ (x_1 + x_2 + \ldots + x_k)^n = \sum_{k_1 + k_2 + \ldots + k_k = n} \frac{n!}{k_1! k_2! \ldots k_k!} x_1^{k_1} x_2^{k_2} \ldots x_k^{k_k} \]
  2. Here, \(n = 9\), \(x_1 = x\), \(x_2 = 2y\), \(x_3 = 3z\), and we need \(k_1 = 2\), \(k_2 = 3\), \(k_3 = 4\).
  3. The coefficient is: \[ \frac{9!}{2!3!4!} (2^3)(3^4) \]
  4. Calculate each part: \[ 9! = 362880, \quad 2! = 2, \quad 3! = 6, \quad 4! = 24 \]
  5. The multinomial coefficient is: \[ \frac{362880}{2 \cdot 6 \cdot 24} = 1260 \]
  6. The numerical part is: \[ (2^3)(3^4) = 8 \cdot 81 = 648 \]
  7. The final coefficient is: \[ 1260 \cdot 648 = 816480 \]
  8. Conclusion: The coefficient of \(x^2y^3z^4\) is 816480.
